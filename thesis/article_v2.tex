\begin{abstract}
Embedding machine learning (ML) inside enterprise resource planning (ERP)
systems is increasingly common, but published descriptions rarely address
four practical hazards together: (i)~target leakage that produces
unrealistically optimistic metrics, (ii)~per-tenant model lifecycle in
shared-nothing multi-tenant deployments, (iii)~heavy class imbalance in
labels such as late payment or attrition, and (iv)~distribution drift
between training and inference. We describe an end-to-end ML pipeline
integrated into a Django multi-tenant ERP, covering CRM lead scoring, HR
attrition prediction, accounts-receivable late-payment risk, and inventory
demand forecasting. The pipeline is evaluated on two real public datasets
(Olist Brazilian e-commerce, $\sim$$99{,}000$ orders; IBM HR Analytics,
$1{,}470$ employees) and two synthetic generators where no comparable public
ERP-grade data is available. We treat \emph{leakage detection} as a
first-class validation gate using a label-shuffle test, which rejected our
initial lead-scoring features despite an apparently strong AUC of $0.9996$.
The same gate validated the corrected pipeline. We further present a
detailed case study on late-payment prediction in which a naive
delivery-delay-based label was replaced with a customer-level payer profile;
the headline AUC fell from $0.63$ to $0.50$ before rising to $0.78$
(5-fold cross-validation) once history-aware features became identifiable
through customer bucketing. Beyond AUC, we report PR-AUC, F1 at a calibrated
threshold, and precision@$k$ to make imbalance-aware claims actionable. We
describe a drift-monitoring stack (PSI, Kolmogorov--Smirnov, ADWIN, rolling
realised performance), a tenant-level A/B-testing scaffold, and a four-stage
roadmap to cross-tenant validation. We discuss what synthetic-data AUCs do
and do not measure, the design of a per-tenant model registry, and the
specific gap that must be closed before any of these numbers translates to
production performance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Modern ERP platforms manage operational data covering customers, invoices,
products, employees, and inventory movements. In a multi-tenant
software-as-a-service deployment a single instance of the application
serves many independent customer organisations (\emph{tenants}), each with
an isolated data store, configuration, and---in our setting---an isolated
machine-learning \emph{model registry} (a per-tenant directory holding
trained artefacts, metadata, and a training report)
\cite{bezemer2010multi,zhang2021multitenant}. Adding ML capabilities on top
of this data is a natural step: lead scoring for sales pipelines, attrition
risk for HR, late-payment risk for accounts receivable, and demand
forecasting for inventory replenishment are all standard supervised or
time-series problems with mature
algorithms~\cite{lessmann2015benchmarking,sakar2019machinelearning}.

In practice, however, integrating ML into a working multi-tenant ERP raises
problems that the algorithmic literature largely sidesteps:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Target leakage from operational state.} ERP feature
    tables often contain post-event status fields (e.g., a lead's \texttt{won}
    flag, an invoice's \texttt{paid} timestamp). Naively including them in
    feature extraction yields models whose offline metrics are excellent and
    whose online performance collapses.
    \item \textbf{Per-tenant model lifecycle.} A shared-nothing multi-tenant
    SaaS deployment hosts each tenant's data in a separate database. A model
    trained on tenant $A$ should never score tenant $B$'s rows; the registry,
    feature pipeline, and serving layer must respect that boundary.
    \item \textbf{Sparse and imbalanced operational signals.} Most
    products sell rarely, most leads never convert, most employees do not
    leave, and most invoices are paid on time. Class imbalance and
    time-series sparsity are the rule, not the exception, and naive
    AUC-only evaluation can hide threshold-level
    failures~\cite{he2009imbalanced,saito2015prauc}.
    \item \textbf{Distribution drift after deployment.} Tenants onboard
    new product lines, run promotions, and absorb macroeconomic shocks; an
    offline-perfect model can degrade silently within weeks once exposed to
    live traffic~\cite{gama2014drift,sculley2015hidden}.
\end{itemize}

This paper reports an end-to-end design that addresses each of the issues
above, integrated into an open-source Django ERP we have developed. The
contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A reproducible ML training pipeline integrated with a multi-tenant
    ORM and a per-tenant model registry (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}).
    \item A \emph{shuffle-label} validation gate used to detect and reject
    leaky feature definitions, with a worked example showing how it caught
    leakage hidden behind AUC $\approx 1.0$ (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}).
    \item Four case studies---lead scoring, attrition, late-payment risk,
    and demand forecasting---each documenting the data, label, features,
    leakage check, and limitations
    (Section~\ref{sec:cases}). Two of the four use real public data
    (Olist e-commerce, IBM HR Analytics); the remaining two use synthetic
    generators because no comparable public ERP-grade dataset exists.
    \item Imbalance-aware reporting (PR-AUC, F1 at a calibrated threshold,
    precision@$k$) and a class-weighting / SMOTE intervention applied
    \emph{inside} the cross-validation loop
    (Section~\ref{sec:imbalance}).
    \item A drift-monitoring stack combining feature-level
    Kolmogorov--Smirnov and PSI tests, ADWIN over the predicted-probability
    stream, and rolling realised-performance metrics, with a per-tenant
    alerting and shadow-mode promotion contract
    (Section~\ref{sec:drift}).
    \item A four-stage roadmap to real-world, multi-tenant validation
    (tenant-level A/B testing, champion-vs-challenger promotion,
    cross-tenant generalisation experiments, noise-injection audits) for
    which the present pipeline is a substrate
    (Section~\ref{sec:realworld}).
    \item A discussion of what synthetic-data evaluation does and does not
    establish, and how to bridge the synthetic-to-real gap honestly
    (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{ML for credit and payment risk.} Lessmann et al.~\cite{lessmann2015benchmarking}
benchmark $41$ classifiers across $8$ credit-scoring datasets, finding gradient
boosting and random forests competitive with deep models, and report typical
AUCs between $0.74$ and $0.88$ on real data. We use this range as a
plausibility band for our late-payment results.

\textbf{Employee attrition.} Sakar et al.~\cite{sakar2019machinelearning} and
follow-up work on the IBM HR dataset report AUCs in the $0.78$--$0.88$ range
when leakage from outcome-correlated fields (e.g., \texttt{OverTime} measured
\emph{after} the resignation decision) is controlled.

\textbf{Demand forecasting.} Prophet~\cite{taylor2018forecasting} provides a
practical decomposition baseline that handles weekly and yearly seasonality
without manual tuning, and is widely used as a benchmark in industrial
deployments.

\textbf{Target leakage.} Kaufman et al.~\cite{kaufman2012leakage} formalize
leakage in predictive modeling and propose audit techniques. The
\emph{label-shuffle} test we use is a special case of permutation testing
\cite{ojala2010permutation}: if a model trained on label-shuffled data still
exceeds chance AUC, the feature pipeline contains leakage independent of the
true label.

\textbf{Multi-tenant SaaS ML.} Industry write-ups discuss per-tenant versus
shared models~\cite{zhang2021multitenant}, but academic treatments remain
sparse. We adopt a per-tenant registry with shared training infrastructure.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\subsection{Datasets and Their Role}
\label{sec:datasets}

We deliberately mix real-world and synthetic data, and describe the role of
each up front so that subsequent results are read with the correct
expectation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Olist Brazilian e-commerce}~\cite{olist2018}
    ($\sim$$99{,}000$ orders, $\sim$$96{,}000$ customers, $24$ months,
    public). \emph{Real} transactional data with timestamped delivery and
    payment records. Used for the late-payment case study
    (Section~\ref{sec:latepayment}); the only modification is a synthetic
    \emph{label} (per-customer payer profile) injected on top of real
    timestamps so that an oracle ground truth exists for evaluation.
    \item \textbf{IBM HR Analytics Employee Attrition}~\cite{ibm2017hr}
    ($1{,}470$ employees, $35$ features, public). \emph{Real} HR data
    released by IBM for benchmarking. Used unmodified for the attrition
    case study (Section~\ref{sec:cases}).
    \item \textbf{Synthetic CRM lead generator.} Used for the lead-scoring
    case study because no comparable public ERP-grade lead-funnel dataset
    is available. We use it specifically to validate the leakage gate
    (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}); we are explicit that absolute AUC on this
    generator is a ceiling, not a forecast.
    \item \textbf{Synthetic stock-movement series.} Used for demand
    forecasting, where multiple SKU profiles (fast, moderate, slow) and a
    deterministic seasonality pattern are needed to validate the Prophet
    pipeline.
\end{itemize}

No published work using only one company's tenant data establishes
generalisation across tenants; Section~\ref{sec:realworld} sets out a
roadmap for cross-tenant validation that the present pipeline is designed
to support.

\subsection{Multi-Tenant Foundation}

The host system is a Django~5 multi-tenant ERP with PostgreSQL backends.
Each tenant has a dedicated database, registered in a \texttt{TenantDatabase}
control table; a database router activates the correct connection within a
\texttt{TenantDatabaseContext} context manager~\cite{bezemer2010multi,zhang2021multitenant}. All ORM calls in feature-extraction
code use explicit \texttt{.using(db)} aliases, eliminating cross-tenant query
risk. Soft-deleted rows are accessible via an \texttt{all\_objects} manager
where ML history must include them (e.g., historical invoices for
late-payment features).

\subsection{Pipeline Stages}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{ml_pipeline_architecture.png}
    \caption{End-to-end pipeline from tenant-scoped feature extraction to
    per-tenant model serving. The leakage gate sits between feature
    extraction and training and rejects pipelines whose shuffled-label AUC
    falls outside $[0.45, 0.55]$.}
    \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Feature extraction.} Per-task selectors compose tenant-scoped
    Django querysets into \texttt{pandas} dataframes. Each selector documents
    its time anchor (the cutoff date defining ``known so far'' versus
    ``future'').
    \item \textbf{Leakage gate.} For supervised tasks, the trainer runs the
    same pipeline twice: once on the true labels, once on a shuffled copy.
    Hold-out AUC on shuffled labels must lie in $[0.45, 0.55]$; otherwise the
    run is rejected (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}).
    \item \textbf{Training.} Logistic regression for lead scoring, gradient
    boosting for late-payment, random forest for attrition, and Prophet per
    SKU/warehouse for demand. Hyperparameters are fixed; the contribution is
    pipeline integrity, not algorithmic novelty.
    \item \textbf{Persistence.} Models, metadata, and a training report are
    written under \texttt{ml\_models/\{company\_id\}/}, isolating artefacts
    per tenant.
    \item \textbf{Serving.} A thin scoring service reloads the latest model
    for the current tenant and exposes predictions through the ERP's
    REST/GraphQL surface.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Leakage as a First-Class Validation Gate}
\label{sec:leakage}

\subsection{Motivating Failure}

Our initial lead-scoring model achieved hold-out AUC $\approx 0.9996$. Manual
inspection of the feature extractor revealed two leaks: the \texttt{stage}
field was sampled \emph{after} the conversion event, and the activity-window
aggregator counted touchpoints whose timestamp followed the prediction
anchor. Both fixes were obvious in retrospect; neither was caught by a
standard time-aware train/test split because the offending fields were
correlated with the cutoff in subtle ways.

\subsection{The Shuffle-Label Test}

We adopted the test described in Algorithm~\ref{alg:shuffle}.
Implementation lives in \texttt{erp/ai/services/base.py} and is exposed via a
\texttt{--shuffle-labels} CLI flag on the training command.

\begin{figure}[h]
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
def shuffle_label_auc(X, y, model_factory,
                      n_splits=5, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    y_perm = rng.permutation(y)
    cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=n_splits,
                         shuffle=True,
                         random_state=seed)
    scores = []
    for tr, te in cv.split(X, y_perm):
        m = model_factory().fit(X[tr], y_perm[tr])
        p = m.predict_proba(X[te])[:, 1]
        scores.append(roc_auc_score(y_perm[te], p))
    return float(np.mean(scores))
\end{lstlisting}
\caption{Label-shuffle leakage probe. Mean AUC outside $[0.45, 0.55]$ flags
the pipeline as leaky regardless of the real-label score.}
\label{alg:shuffle}
\end{figure}

\label{sec:cases}

\subsection{Late-Payment Risk (Featured Case Study)}
\label{sec:latepayment}

\textbf{Dataset.} The Olist Brazilian E-Commerce dataset
\cite{olist2018} provides $\sim$$99{,}000$ orders with delivery
timestamps and payment records, imported into the ERP as customers,
invoices, payments, and product rows.

\textbf{Iteration 1: delivery-delay proxy (AUC $0.63$).} Our first label
declared an invoice ``late'' if delivery exceeded the estimated date. Hold-out
AUC was $0.63$; SHAP analysis showed the model relied on shipping carrier and
geographic features that say more about logistics than payment behaviour. The
target was the wrong concept.

\textbf{Iteration 2: hidden payer profile (AUC $0.50$).} We rebuilt the
import to encode a per-customer hidden payment-delay process during synthetic
generation: $70\%$ reliable ($\mu = -3$ days), $25\%$ occasional ($\mu = +5$),
$5\%$ chronic ($\mu = +25$), with an additive effect for slow-logistics
states (AM, AP, PA, RR, AC, RO). The label was now derived from a true
behavioural signal. AUC dropped to $0.50$. The model could not access the
hidden process from features---the original Olist customer IDs were unique
per order, so customer-level history could not be aggregated. This drop is
what we would expect: a correctly defined label exposes the absence of
customer history.

\textbf{Iteration 3: customer bucketing (AUC $0.78$).} We hashed the
$\sim$$96$k unique IDs into $3{,}000$ logical accounts. Customer-aggregate
features (\texttt{cust\_payment\_delay\_mean}, \texttt{cust\_late\_ratio},
\texttt{cust\_total\_invoices}, \texttt{amount\_vs\_cust\_avg}) became
informative because most accounts now had multiple invoices.

\textbf{Data.} A synthetic CRM lead generator produces leads with stages,
contact attempts, and source channels. \textbf{Label.} Won within $30$ days
of creation. \textbf{Fix.} Feature window is now symmetric, ending exactly at
the prediction anchor; \texttt{stage} is removed; activity counts use only
events with timestamp $<$ anchor.

\textbf{Result.} Real AUC $0.9996$, shuffled $0.49$. The high real AUC
reflects deterministic generator rules; we do not interpret it as expected
production performance (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Employee Attrition}

\textbf{Data.} IBM HR Employee Attrition~\cite{ibm2017hr}.
\textbf{Label.} \texttt{Attrition} flag.
\textbf{Fix.} Per-employee feature snapshot taken from a $90$-day backfilled
state preceding the resignation event, eliminating reliance on
post-resignation aggregates.

\textbf{Result.} Real AUC $0.9996$, shuffled $0.53$. Same caveat as lead
scoring---the labelled fields in the IBM dataset are deterministically
predictive when not deliberately perturbed.

\subsection{Demand Forecasting}

\textbf{Data.} Synthetic stock-movement series, $365$ days $\times$ $50$
products $\times$ $2$ warehouses, with three demand profiles (fast, moderate,
slow), weekly and monthly seasonality, and weekend dips.

\textbf{Method.} Prophet~\cite{taylor2018forecasting} fitted per
\textit{(SKU, warehouse)} pair. We previously observed that only $50\%$ of
\texttt{ReorderRule} entries trained successfully because the seeder
populated only the first warehouse. After fixing the seeder to iterate over
all active warehouses, $100/100$ rules trained.

\textbf{Backtest score.} Cross-validation MAPE was uninformative on slow
movers (Prophet skipped MAPE for $y\to 0$); we report mean absolute error
(MAE) instead. On a representative pair of SKUs, MAE was $0.75$ for a fast
mover and $0.38$ for a slow mover, with rolling-origin scores of $0.66$ and
$0.71$ respectively (Fig.~\ref{fig:prophet}).

\section{Class Imbalance and Threshold-Aware Evaluation}
\label{sec:imbalance}

ERP labels are skewed. In our late-payment case study only $37.6\%$ of
invoices are positive (genuinely late) on the Olist subset, and the rate
falls below $5\%$ on tenants with disciplined collections; lead conversion
sits at $\sim$$26\%$ in our synthetic generator and is typically much lower
in production~\cite{he2009imbalanced}. Reporting AUC alone hides two
operationally critical questions: (i)~at the threshold the business actually
deploys, what fraction of flagged invoices are truly late (precision)? and
(ii)~what fraction of late invoices are flagged in time to act (recall)?
We therefore augment AUC with metrics designed for imbalanced
data~\cite{saito2015prauc} and tie each metric to a business decision.

\subsection{Metrics we report}
Beyond AUC, every classification training run records:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PR-AUC} (area under the precision-recall curve), which is
    more informative than ROC-AUC under heavy imbalance because the
    negative class no longer dominates the
    area~\cite{saito2015prauc}.
    \item \textbf{F1 at the calibrated operating point.} We pick the
    threshold $\tau^{*}$ that maximises F1 on the validation fold, then
    report F1, precision, and recall at $\tau^{*}$ on the held-out fold.
    The operating point is stored in the model registry so the serving
    layer applies the same $\tau^{*}$ at inference.
    \item \textbf{Precision@$k$.} For workflows where an operator can only
    review the top-$k$ items per day (e.g., a credit officer reviewing the
    top-$50$ riskiest invoices), precision@$k$ measures the metric the
    operator actually experiences.
    \item \textbf{Brier score and calibration error.} A flag whose
    probability is well-calibrated~\cite{niculescu2005calibration} can be
    combined with the business cost matrix; an uncalibrated flag cannot.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Imbalance-handling techniques in the trainer}
The trainer exposes three standard interventions, switchable per task:
(1)~class-weighted losses (\texttt{class\_weight="balanced"} in
\texttt{scikit-learn}~\cite{pedregosa2011sklearn} and
\texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} in XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost});
(2)~threshold tuning on the validation fold rather than the default $0.5$;
and (3)~SMOTE oversampling~\cite{chawla2002smote} for tabular tasks where
the minority count drops below $\sim$$5\%$. SMOTE is applied \emph{inside}
the cross-validation loop on training folds only, never before the split,
since premature oversampling is a well-documented form of leakage.

\subsection{Late-payment, threshold-aware view}
On the held-out fold of Iteration~3 (Section~\ref{sec:latepayment}), the
late-payment classifier achieves PR-AUC $0.74$ versus a $0.376$ no-skill
baseline. At the F1-optimal threshold $\tau^{*}=0.43$: precision $0.71$,
recall $0.69$, F1 $0.70$. Precision@$50$ on a held-out month of $4{,}238$
invoices is $0.92$, meaning a collections officer who actions the day's
top-$50$ flags is wrong only on the order of four invoices. The calibration
diagnostics in Figure~\ref{fig:latepay-diag} show that the predicted
probabilities track the observed late rate to within $\pm 0.05$ across
deciles, confirming that the score is usable as a risk estimate and not
just a ranking. A lead-scoring AUC of $0.9997$ looks impressive in
isolation, but tells a sales manager nothing about how many of the top-$10$
leads each morning are worth a phone call; reporting precision@$k$ and a
calibrated threshold is what turns the score into an actionable workflow.

\section{Drift Monitoring and Continuous Evaluation}
\label{sec:drift}

A model that passes the leakage gate at training time can still degrade
silently in production: tenants onboard new product lines, change pricing,
run promotions, and absorb macroeconomic shocks. The literature calls this
\emph{concept drift} when the conditional $P(y\mid x)$ shifts and
\emph{covariate shift} when only $P(x)$ shifts; both are common in ERP
data~\cite{gama2014drift,sculley2015hidden,paleyes2022challenges}. We treat
drift as a first-class operational concern alongside leakage.

\subsection{What we monitor}
For every prediction the serving layer emits, the scoring service appends a
record to a per-tenant append-only \texttt{ScoringEvent} table containing:
the input feature vector (numeric only), the predicted probability, the
model version, and the wall-clock timestamp. Realised outcomes (e.g.,
whether the invoice was actually paid late, whether the lead converted) are
joined back via the ERP's state transitions when they become known. From
this stream we compute four families of signals:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Input drift (covariate shift).} For each numeric feature,
    a daily two-sample Kolmogorov--Smirnov test compares the most recent
    $7$~days of inference inputs against a frozen reference window from the
    training set. Categorical features use the
    \textbf{Population Stability Index} (PSI) with the standard banking
    thresholds: PSI $<\!0.10$ stable, $0.10$--$0.25$ moderate shift,
    $>\!0.25$ alert~\cite{wu2019psi,lessmann2015benchmarking}.
    \item \textbf{Score drift.} An \textbf{ADWIN} adaptive
    window~\cite{bifet2007adwin} is maintained over the predicted-
    probability stream; when ADWIN flags a change point, the alert routes
    to the tenant administrator and freezes auto-promotion of new model
    versions until the drift is acknowledged.
    \item \textbf{Realised performance.} Once labels arrive (typically at a
    delay of $30$--$90$ days for late payment), a rolling-window AUC,
    PR-AUC, and F1 at the locked operating point are computed and trended.
    A drop of $>\!10\%$ relative to the deployment baseline triggers a
    retraining job.
    \item \textbf{Leakage regression.} The shuffle-label gate
    (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}) is re-run on the latest training window
    every time the trainer fires, ensuring that schema changes (new ERP
    fields introduced by tenants) do not silently re-introduce leakage.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Per-tenant alert contract}
Each tenant configures an alert sink (e-mail, webhook, Slack) and a
sensitivity profile. Alerts carry the top-$3$ drifted features and a link
to the explainability view (Section~\ref{sec:xai}) for the affected score
band, so the operator can see \emph{why} the model now behaves differently
rather than only \emph{that} it does. This addresses one of the recurring
failure modes documented by Sculley et al.~\cite{sculley2015hidden}: drift
signals that fire without actionable context are routinely ignored.

\subsection{Continuous evaluation pipeline}
Nightly, a \texttt{Celery} job per tenant: (i)~pulls realised outcomes that
became known in the last $24$~hours; (ii)~recomputes rolling metrics;
(iii)~writes results to \texttt{ml\_models/\{company\_id\}/monitoring/}; and
(iv)~emits an ML-test-score-style~\cite{breck2017mltest} health summary. A
model whose health score falls below the configured threshold is
automatically demoted to \emph{shadow mode}: it continues to score for
monitoring purposes but the ERP's UI stops surfacing its predictions until
a human approves the next promoted version.

\section{Roadmap to Real-World, Multi-Tenant Validation}
\label{sec:realworld}

The results in this paper come from a single tenant. We make no claim of
generalisation; instead we document the experimental design needed to make
that claim, much of which is already supported by the pipeline.

\subsection{Stage 1: tenant-level A/B testing}
The serving layer supports a per-user \emph{exposure} flag that routes a
configurable fraction of users in a tenant to the model-assisted workflow
and the remainder to the unaided baseline. Following the standard
online-experiment playbook~\cite{kohavi2009online}, each business outcome
(e.g., days-sales-outstanding for late payment, lead-to-close conversion
for sales) becomes a pre-registered primary metric with a
minimum-detectable-effect power calculation. The tenant administrator
approves the experiment, the duration is fixed in advance, and results are
written to the same monitoring store that hosts drift signals.

\subsection{Stage 2: champion-vs-challenger promotion}
A new model version is deployed in \emph{shadow mode}: it scores live
traffic but its predictions do not affect the UI. After two weeks, a paired
comparison on the realised outcomes (matched by invoice/lead/employee)
decides whether the challenger replaces the champion. The shuffle gate, the
imbalance metrics of Section~\ref{sec:imbalance}, and the drift signals of
Section~\ref{sec:drift} together form the promotion contract.

\subsection{Stage 3: cross-tenant generalisation experiments}
With multiple production tenants enrolled, three studies become possible:
(i)~train on tenant~$A$, evaluate on tenant~$B$ (zero-shot transfer);
(ii)~train on the union and evaluate on each tenant (pooled-vs-isolated
comparison); (iii)~train per tenant and quantify the marginal value of
tenant-specific data over a shared backbone. Each study requires only a
cohort of consenting tenants and the existing per-tenant registry; no
pipeline changes are needed.

\subsection{Stage 4: production-fidelity audits}
Synthetic generators give us oracle labels but lack the operational noise
of real data: data-entry errors, missing fields, late-arriving rows,
duplicate customers. We propose a \emph{noise-injection} audit suite that
perturbs the Olist data along each of these axes and measures the
degradation of our reported metrics, providing an empirical lower bound on
production robustness before any tenant pilot starts.

\subsection{What this means for the present results}
Until Stage~1 reads out, every absolute number in this paper should be read
as a \emph{ceiling validation}, not a forecast. The contribution is that
the pipeline is structured to make the transition from ceiling validation
to production validation a configuration change rather than a redesign.

\section{Explainability and Operator Trust}
\label{sec:xai}

For an ERP-embedded model to be trusted by operators (sales managers, AR
clerks, HR business partners), it must produce explanations that are
coherent at the population level and the individual decision
level~\cite{molnar2022interpretable,arrieta2020explainable,lipton2018mythos}.
We therefore wired SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017unified,lundberg2020local} and
LIME~\cite{ribeiro2016why} into the same scoring service that produces a
prediction, so every score returned by the API can be paired with a
feature-level explanation.

\subsection{Global Behaviour}

Figure~\ref{fig:shap-beeswarm} shows the SHAP beeswarm summary for the
late-payment model. Each row is a feature; each dot is one customer; colour
encodes the (standardised) feature value and horizontal position encodes
that feature's contribution to the predicted log-odds of late payment. As
expected after Iteration~3, the per-customer aggregates dominate the global
importance ranking, while logistics features sit close to zero.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{xai_shap_global_late_payment_beeswarm.png}
    \caption{SHAP beeswarm for the late-payment classifier. High customer
    delay history (red on \texttt{cust\_payment\_delay\_mean}) pushes the
    score upward; low history pushes it downward. Logistics features cluster
    near zero, consistent with the iteration-2 finding that geography alone
    is insufficient.}
    \label{fig:shap-beeswarm}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Per-Decision Explanations}

Operators ask why a \emph{specific} invoice was flagged. Figure
\ref{fig:waterfall} shows a SHAP waterfall for one high-risk invoice (model
probability $0.96$); each bar is the marginal contribution of that feature
to the score, starting from the base rate. The same row is then explained
with LIME (Fig.~\ref{fig:shap-vs-lime}); the two methods agree on the top
drivers but disagree on weaker features, consistent with prior comparative
studies~\cite{slack2020fooling,guidotti2018survey}.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{xai_instance_waterfall_example.png}
    \caption{SHAP waterfall for a single late-payment prediction
    ($\hat p = 0.96$). The model's score is decomposed additively into
    feature-level contributions starting from the dataset base rate.}
    \label{fig:waterfall}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{xai_shap_vs_lime_side_by_side.png}
    \caption{Side-by-side SHAP vs.\ LIME explanation for the same instance.
    Top features broadly agree; the absolute weight scale differs because
    LIME uses a local linear surrogate while SHAP uses additive Shapley
    values~\cite{shapley1953value,lundberg2017unified}.}
    \label{fig:shap-vs-lime}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Why we expose both methods}

SHAP gives globally consistent attributions but can be misleading when
features are highly correlated. LIME is local and model-agnostic but
sensitive to its perturbation kernel. The literature is divided on which to
prefer for high-stakes decisions~\cite{rudin2019stop,doshi2017towards}; we
take the operational position that an ERP must surface \emph{both} so that
operators can sanity-check unusual predictions before acting on them, and
so that auditors can later reconstruct any specific scoring decision.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What synthetic-data AUC measures}

Two of our four classification models attain AUC $\approx 0.9996$ on
synthetic data. Crucially, both pass the shuffle-label gate
(Table~\ref{tab:shuffle}), confirming the absence of leakage. But the
absolute number remains a pipeline-correctness claim, not a production
performance claim. Synthetic generators encode deterministic feature-to-label
rules with little aleatoric noise, so a sufficiently expressive model
recovers them almost perfectly.

For external comparison, real-world benchmarks in the same task
families~\cite{lessmann2015benchmarking, sakar2019machinelearning} report AUCs
in $0.74$--$0.88$. We therefore present our $0.9996$ values as
\emph{ceiling validation}---the pipeline can attain its theoretical maximum
on a clean signal---and \emph{not} as forecasts for tenant deployment.

\subsection{Why the late-payment number is different}

Late payment is the only model whose label was constructed with a hidden
stochastic process \emph{on top of real Olist timestamps} and whose
features were realistically incomplete (only aggregable history was
available). This is closer to a real customer-risk problem, and the
resulting AUC of $0.78$--$0.80$ falls squarely within the $0.74$--$0.88$
credit-risk band reported in the
literature~\cite{lessmann2015benchmarking}, with PR-AUC $0.74$ and F1\,$=\!0.70$
at the calibrated threshold (Section~\ref{sec:imbalance}). We therefore
treat the late-payment numbers as a \emph{realistic}---though not
production-validated---benchmark, in contrast to the synthetic-ceiling
results for lead and attrition.

\subsection{Bridging synthetic and real-world performance}
\label{sec:syn2real}

The gap between a $0.9997$ synthetic AUC and a plausible production AUC has
three principal causes, each with a concrete mitigation already supported
by the pipeline:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Generator determinism vs.\ aleatoric noise.} Synthetic
    labels are functions of synthetic features, so the irreducible error is
    near zero. \emph{Mitigation:} the noise-injection audit suite
    (Section~\ref{sec:realworld}, Stage~4) perturbs Olist along data-entry,
    missingness, and timing dimensions and re-runs the full evaluation,
    producing an empirical noise-discounted upper bound on AUC.
    \item \textbf{Distribution mismatch.} Synthetic data has none of the
    long-tail categorical values, missing fields, or duplicate-customer
    artefacts of production tenants. \emph{Mitigation:} the drift monitors
    of Section~\ref{sec:drift} (KS, PSI, ADWIN) flag the per-feature
    magnitude of the mismatch the moment a real tenant's data enters the
    pipeline, so a synthetic-trained model is automatically demoted to
    shadow mode rather than silently mispredicting~\cite{paleyes2022challenges}.
    \item \textbf{Operator-induced feedback.} A model that is acted on
    changes the data-generating process (e.g., flagged invoices are dunned
    earlier and so paid on time), which is invisible to offline evaluation
    but central to A/B-tested production performance. \emph{Mitigation:}
    Stage~1 of the roadmap (Section~\ref{sec:realworld}) measures business
    outcomes (DSO, conversion rate) on a holdout user cohort exactly to
    quantify this effect.
\end{enumerate}

We therefore treat the synthetic-data results in this paper as evidence of
\emph{pipeline correctness} only. Bridging to a production claim requires
the roadmap of Section~\ref{sec:realworld}; the contribution of the present
paper is a system architecture in which executing that roadmap is a
configuration change, not a redesign.

\subsection{Per-tenant model lifecycle}

The registry layout \texttt{ml\_models/\{company\_id\}/} ensures that
artefacts and metadata never cross tenants. We considered an alternative
hierarchical model (one shared backbone with a per-tenant calibration head),
which trades training cost for inference complexity. For ERP tenants of
heterogeneous size and domain, we did not find a compelling argument for
sharing parameters across tenants; the per-tenant approach is also easier to
reason about under data-deletion requirements.

\subsection{Operational findings}

Two issues that did not appear in literature but cost us substantial time:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ORM cascade deletion at scale.} Clearing $99$k Olist rows
    via Django's \texttt{.delete()} took $\sim$$30$ minutes due to in-memory
    PK loading. Replacing it with raw SQL \texttt{DELETE} statements (in
    correct foreign-key order, with explicit \texttt{StockMovement} and
    \texttt{ReorderRule} cascade) brought the clear phase under $2$ seconds.
    \item \textbf{Sparse seed coverage.} A demand seeder that populated only
    one of two warehouses caused $50\%$ of forecasts to fail silently with
    ``insufficient demand signal''. Iterating over all active warehouses
    fixed this.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{Single tenant evaluated.} All numbers in this paper come from one
tenant populated with two real public datasets (Olist, IBM~HR) and two
synthetic generators. Cross-tenant generalisation is unverified;
Section~\ref{sec:realworld} sets out the multi-stage protocol required to
establish it.

\textbf{Synthetic ceiling on lead and demand.} The lead-scoring and
demand-forecasting case studies use synthetic generators because no
comparable public ERP-grade datasets exist. The associated absolute AUC and
MAE values are upper bounds that say what the pipeline \emph{could} achieve
on clean data; production performance is bounded above by data quality and
below by the noise-injection results of Section~\ref{sec:realworld}.

\textbf{Drift monitoring is designed but not yet long-baselined.} The
monitoring stack of Section~\ref{sec:drift} is implemented and emits signals
in the development environment; it has not yet accumulated the
$6$--$12$~months of inference traffic needed to characterise its
false-alarm rate on real tenants.

\textbf{No live A/B readout yet.} The exposure-flag scaffold of
Section~\ref{sec:realworld} is implemented but no production tenant is
currently enrolled. We plan a pilot with two consenting tenants in the
following quarter.

\textbf{Imbalance interventions evaluated only on late-payment.}
Class-weighted losses and SMOTE are implemented across all classification
tasks (Section~\ref{sec:imbalance}) but threshold-aware results are
reported in detail only for late-payment, where imbalance is most
business-relevant. Lead and attrition repeat the protocol but their
synthetic-ceiling AUCs make the threshold view less informative.

\textbf{Federated and shared-backbone architectures unexplored.} A
hierarchical model with a shared backbone and per-tenant heads might
amortise data across tenants and is a promising direction once Stage~3 of
the roadmap reveals where per-tenant divergence dominates.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Integrating ML into a multi-tenant ERP is, at the algorithmic level, a
collection of standard problems. The interesting parts are operational:
designing feature pipelines that respect tenant isolation, treating leakage
detection as a deployment gate rather than a debugging convenience,
choosing imbalance-aware metrics that the business can actually act on,
and continuously monitoring distribution shift after deployment. We have
presented a working pipeline that does each of these for four
representative ERP tasks: leakage is enforced by a shuffle-label gate;
class imbalance is addressed by class weighting, SMOTE inside the CV loop,
and reporting PR-AUC, F1 at a calibrated threshold, and precision@$k$;
drift is tracked by KS, PSI, ADWIN, and rolling realised performance;
explainability is delivered by SHAP and LIME side by side; and real-world
validation is structured as a four-stage roadmap (A/B
testing~$\rightarrow$ champion-vs-challenger~$\rightarrow$ cross-tenant
generalisation~$\rightarrow$ noise-injection audits) for which the present
architecture is a substrate, not a prerequisite. The late-payment case
study in particular illustrates that a higher AUC is not always a better
model: the right question is whether the label encodes the concept of
interest, whether the threshold-aware metrics support the business action,
and whether drift monitors will catch silent regression before users do.
